---
title: Good Editing Knows When to Stop
date: 2026-10-06
excerpt: Video editing and writing look like different crafts. Both get better when you stop trying to prove how much you can add.
prompt: Write about one belief that two of your most different interests secretly share.
---

The easiest way to make a color grade look impressive is to keep pushing it. More contrast. More saturation. A cooler shadow, a warmer face, a little extra something in the sky. You can keep going until the image announces that someone worked on it.

That doesn't mean the image got better.

I shoot and edit video, and I prefer a natural, restrained grade. The goal isn't to make every shot look like it came from a different, more dramatic world. The goal is to help the footage feel like itself. Sometimes that means a change. Sometimes it means taking a change back. And sometimes the right move is to stop before the software gives you another reason to keep moving sliders.

Writing has the same trap. A sentence can be made louder without being made clearer. Add another adjective. Explain the point again in different words. Put a big, shiny conclusion at the end because the paragraph seems like it should have one. The work starts to show its machinery. You can feel the writer reaching for an effect.

Video and writing share a belief I keep coming back to: the craft should make the meaning easier to feel, not compete with it.

In an edit, every choice takes up attention. A cut changes the pace. Music tells you how to feel before you have decided what you feel. A mask can guide the eye toward one part of the frame and away from another. None of those things is automatically bad. They are tools. But a tool can become the subject if I use it just to show that I know how.

Color is especially good at exposing that. The controls invite you to make a visible decision. Move the temperature, and the image changes. Pull the saturation down, and it looks more serious. Push it up, and it feels more alive—until skin looks wrong and the scene stops feeling like the place it was filmed. A restrained grade isn't the absence of judgment. It's judgment applied to the point where the image still feels believable.

Writing has its own sliders. The sentence can be longer, the metaphor more clever, the opening more dramatic. Those moves can help. But if the reader notices the sentence more than the idea, the sentence may be doing too much. A clean sentence isn't a small ambition. It is a decision to leave room for the thought.

The temptation in both crafts is to confuse visible effort with value. If I spend time on a shot, I want the shot to display the time. If I spend an hour on a paragraph, I want the paragraph to display the hour. But the audience doesn't owe me credit for labor they can't see. Their experience is the work. If the image lands naturally or the thought arrives cleanly, the invisible decisions did their job.

That can be difficult because stopping feels like giving something up. You notice one more improvement you could make. A little more polish. A little more personality. A little more proof that you care. The useful question isn't “Could I add something?” Of course I could. It's “Would the work be more honest if I did?”

I don't think restraint means keeping everything plain. It doesn't mean avoiding style or being afraid of strong choices. Sometimes the right cut is sharp. Sometimes color should be bold. Sometimes a sentence should hit hard. Restraint isn't a preset. It's knowing what the moment can carry and not asking it to carry more just because you can.

That is what makes editing and writing feel connected to me. In both, I want the choices to serve the thing I'm trying to show. I want the viewer to notice the scene, not my grade. I want the reader to notice the idea, not my performance of having one.

The best decision may be the one nobody can point to. Not because it wasn't there, but because it made everything else feel more itself.
